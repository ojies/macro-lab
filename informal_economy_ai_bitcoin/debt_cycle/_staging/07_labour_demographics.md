## CRITICAL METHODOLOGY BREAK (read first)

**Nigeria's unemployment series has a structural break between 2020 and 2023. The two series MUST NEVER be compared naively.**

| | OLD series (13th ICLS) | NEW series (19th ICLS / NLFS) |
|---|---|---|
| Framework | 13th ICLS guidelines | 19th ICLS, ILO-aligned |
| Survey vehicle | NBS "Labour Force Statistics — Unemployment & Underemployment Report" | Nigeria Labour Force Survey (NLFS) |
| Last / first datapoint | **Last: Q4 2020 (33.3%)** | **First published: Aug 2023, back-cast to Q4 2022 (5.3%)** |
| Definition of *employed* | Worked **≥ 20 hours/week** for pay/profit | Worked **≥ 1 hour** in last 7 days for pay/profit |
| Treatment of "1–19 hrs" | Counted as **unemployed** (inflated the rate) | Counted as **employed** |
| Subsistence agriculture | Counted as employed | Counted as **outside the labour force** |
| Headline result | 33.3% (Q4 2020) | 4.1% (Q1 2023) |

**Why the rate "collapsed" from 33.3% to ~4–5%:** under the old rule, anyone working under 20 hours a week was classified *unemployed*; the new ILO rule classifies anyone working ≥1 hour as *employed*. In a labour market that is ~90%+ informal and dominated by low-hours self-employment, this single redefinition moved millions out of "unemployed". The headline drop is **almost entirely a definitional artefact, not a real improvement in jobs.** The NBS produced **no methodologically comparable bridge** between the two series, and there was **no national survey for 2019, 2021 or 2022** under either method — a genuine data gap. The new low rate is also widely criticised (Proshare, BusinessDay, African Business) because it counts sub-minimum-wage menial work as employment, so the **underemployment and informal-employment shares are the more meaningful distress indicators now.**
