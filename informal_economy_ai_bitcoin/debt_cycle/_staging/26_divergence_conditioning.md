# Why the Development-Age Analogy Is CONDITIONAL — Nigeria-now vs the Catch-up Successes at the Equivalent Stage

**Compiled:** 2026-07-04 · **Analyst pull/access date:** 2026-07-04
**Purpose:** the "conditioning layer" for the development-age model. The naive move is "Nigeria's GDP/capita ≈ the US in 1900 (or Korea in the 1960s) → Nigeria follows that path." This file resists that extrapolation. For each of seven structural factors it compares **Nigeria's initial conditions today** with those of the historical catch-up economies (US, UK, Germany, Japan, South Korea, China) **when they were at Nigeria's current stage** (early/lower-middle income), and states the **direction each factor tilts Nigeria's catch-up odds**.

**Where Nigeria sits now (the anchor):** World Bank **lower-middle-income**, GNI/capita ~US$900 (Atlas, post-2023-24 naira float) but ~US$6,400 PPP — i.e. early-middle-income, the band from which the analogues launched their high-growth decades. Companion file: `_staging/25_*` / `ANALOGUE_CALIBRATION.md` (macro deleveraging analogues); this file adds the **structural / initial-conditions** layer.

**Fairness rule applied throughout:** the analogues were NOT peaceful, clean, or democratic when they took off. The US had a civil war and frontier violence; Korea had a devastating war and an authoritarian, corrupt regime; China had Maoist upheaval. The honest question is not "were they nicer than Nigeria" — it is **how their conditions were SEQUENCED and STRUCTURED relative to their growth phase**, versus Nigeria's today.

**Bottom line up front:** six of seven factors tilt **adverse**; the seventh (demographics) is **adverse-now with latent favourable optionality**. The conditioning pushes Nigeria's analogy away from a "Korea (escape)" reading and toward a **stall / slow-grind** reading — unless several conditions flip together.

---

## 1. Security / internal conflict — **ADVERSE (weight 3)**

**Nigeria now.** Active jihadist insurgency (Boko Haram / ISWAP-JAS) in the North-East, large-scale "banditry" and mass kidnapping in the North-West, and farmer-herder violence across the Middle Belt. ACLED political-violence fatalities (battles + violence against civilians + explosions/remote violence + mob violence) totalled **~9,896 in 2024 and ~12,881 in 2025** — a decade high, concentrated in Borno (3,863 in 2025), Zamfara (1,984), Katsina (1,435), Benue (895), Niger (628), Sokoto (585), Plateau (574) (source: `_staging/22_acled_state_fatalities.md`, direct ACLED-via-HDX aggregation). The state does not hold a monopoly on force across large rural areas of the North.

**Analogues at the equivalent stage.** They were violent too — this must be said plainly:
- **US:** frontier/Indian wars and a catastrophic **Civil War (1861-65)**, ~620k+ dead.
- **South Korea:** the **Korean War (1950-53)** flattened the peninsula — *then* came decades of internal order.
- **China:** the Great Leap famine and Cultural Revolution — *then* the ordered, secure post-1978 reform era.

**Why it still tilts adverse (the sequencing point).** In every analogue the **high-growth phase FOLLOWED the restoration of a state monopoly on violence**. Korea's export miracle ran in the stable decades *after* 1953; China's boom ran *after* Mao. Nigeria's insecurity is **concurrent with its would-be takeoff**, is **territorial** (the state has lost writ over regions), and directly suppresses the very inputs catch-up needs: it depresses agricultural output in the food-basket Middle Belt and North-West, deters domestic and foreign investment, destroys human capital (school closures, kidnappings), and diverts fiscal resources to security. Violence you exit before you grow is a precondition met; violence that runs *through* your growth window is a tax on it.

## 2. Institutions / governance — **ADVERSE (weight 3)**

**Nigeria now.** Worldwide Governance Indicators **Rule of Law = -1.01 (2024)**, down from -0.93 (2023) — roughly the **12th-15th global percentile**; Control of Corruption and Government Effectiveness sit similarly around **-1**. Non-oil tax revenue/GDP is among the world's lowest; contract enforcement and property registration are weak. Corruption here is **rent-extractive** and pairs with **low state capacity**.

**Analogues at the equivalent stage.** Also corrupt — but differently structured:
- **South Korea (Park, 1960s-70s):** authoritarian and graft-ridden, BUT built on a **Weberian bureaucracy** (the Economic Planning Board) with **"embedded / growth-enhancing" corruption** — state rents (cheap credit, protection) were handed to chaebol **conditional on hitting export targets**, so rents were recycled into productive, competitive capacity. GDP/capita went ~$79 (1960) to ~$1,600 (1979) at ~9.7%/yr.
- **US (Gilded Age):** machine politics, robber barons and graft — but atop **secure property rights, functioning courts and rule of law** that protected investment and enforced contracts.

**Why it tilts adverse.** The decisive variable is not the *presence* of corruption (all had it) but its **type and the surrounding state capacity**. Korea's rents were performance-disciplined and reinvested at home; Nigeria's tend to be **extracted and expatriated** (capital flight) with no reciprocal productivity obligation, and they sit on a bureaucracy that cannot reliably tax, enforce contracts, or deliver public goods. Same corruption "score," opposite growth consequence.

## 3. Resource dependence / "resource curse" — **ADVERSE (weight 3)**

**Nigeria now.** **Oil ~88% of merchandise exports and roughly half of federation revenue** (2024). Classic **Dutch disease**: oil rents bias the real exchange rate and fiscal incentives against tradable agriculture and manufacturing, and give the state income *without* needing a productive, taxed citizenry — a rentier fiscal base.

**Analogues at the equivalent stage.** The instructive contrast is the **Asian tigers, who were resource-POOR** (Korea, Taiwan) — a **hidden blessing**: with nothing to dig up and sell, they were *forced* to earn foreign exchange by exporting **manufactures**, which built exactly the competitive tradable sector that drives catch-up. The US/UK/Germany *did* have coal and iron — but as **inputs feeding an industrial base**, not as a rentier substitute *for* one.

**Why it tilts adverse.** Resource poverty catalysed the tigers; resource wealth traps Nigeria. Oil weakens the tax-accountability bargain, funds patronage rather than capability, and crowds out the non-oil tradables that are the engine of convergence. This factor also **causally reinforces factors 2 (institutions) and 6 (deindustrialization)**.

## 4. Demographics — **ADVERSE now, with latent favourable optionality (weight 2)**

**Nigeria now.** TFR **~4.4-5.0**, **median age ~17-18**, population growth **~2.4%/yr**, ~240m people. The demographic dividend is **NOT yet captured** because fertility has fallen only *slowly*, so the **dependency ratio stays high** and per-capita gains are diluted by sheer numbers.

**Analogues at the equivalent stage.** **Korea's TFR fell from 6.1 (1960) to ~2.0 (1982)** — a sharp, policy-assisted transition that produced a **large working-age bulge carrying few dependents**; ~one-third of the East Asian "miracle" growth is attributed to this fast fertility drop. **China** engineered the same via its one-child era. The dividend is a *timing* phenomenon: it pays out only when fertility falls **fast** relative to the working-age bulge.

**Why it tilts adverse now — but could flip.** This is the genuinely **double-edged** factor:
- *Adverse today:* slow fertility decline means Nigeria's dividend is **delayed and shallow**, and fast population growth turns the youth bulge into **unemployment and instability** rather than output — especially with no job-creating industrial sector (see factor 6). The dividend is currently being **squandered**, not captured.
- *Latent upside:* the young population is **raw dividend fuel**, and Nigeria's **~240m-person domestic market** is a structural advantage the small tigers never had (closer to the US/China scale advantage). **If** fertility falls faster **and** jobs materialise, this is the one factor that could turn favourable. That is a big conditional "if."

## 5. Human capital — **ADVERSE (weight 3)**

**Nigeria now.** **~18.3m out-of-school children** (10.2m primary + 8.1m secondary age, UNICEF 2024) — among the world's largest such stocks; **~74% of children aged 7-14 lack basic reading and math skills**; **life expectancy ~62**.

**Analogues at the equivalent stage.** **Korea at its ~1960 takeoff had ~72% adult literacy and reached near-universal primary enrolment by the late 1960s** (built on post-war land reform and mass "education fever"); **China entered its 1978+ reform era with near-universal primary schooling and ~66% literacy**. Both launched high growth with a **broadly literate, healthy workforce ready to absorb factory work and climb a skill ladder**.

**Why it tilts adverse.** Catch-up runs on a labour force that can be *upskilled*. Korea and China had that foundation *before* takeoff; Nigeria's out-of-school stock and foundational-skills deficit mean much of the labour force is **not yet equipped** to raise productivity even if the jobs existed. Human capital is a *stock* — it takes a generation to build, so the gap is slow to close.

## 6. Premature deindustrialization / urbanization-without-industrialization — **ADVERSE (weight 3)**

**Nigeria now.** Manufacturing is **~8-9% of GDP** (fell from ~9.3% in 2023 on the narrow measure); **services ~46-58%** and rising; agriculture ~24%. Nigeria is **urbanizing and shifting to services WITHOUT ever building a manufacturing phase** (in 2023 alone ~767 factories reportedly closed amid power, financing and logistics costs).

**Analogues at the equivalent stage.** **Korea and China industrialized FIRST** — manufacturing reached roughly **25-35% of GDP** at peak, pulling rural labour into **rising-productivity factory jobs** that were **tradable and scalable**, before either pivoted to services.

**Why it tilts adverse (Rodrik).** Rodrik's **premature deindustrialization**: the manufacturing "escalator" now **peaks earlier and at far lower income** for late-comers than it did for early industrializers — the hump has shifted down and toward the origin. Nigeria is losing (never gained) the one sector that historically delivered **scalable, tradable, productivity-raising mass employment**. Urbanization-without-industrialization produces **informal, low-productivity services** — hairdressers and hawkers, not the export factories that generated convergence. This removes the classic catch-up mechanism outright.

---

## 7. Global context — **ADVERSE (weight 2)**

**Nigeria now.** Faces **deglobalization** (world trade/GDP plateaued and drifted down after its ~2008 peak; China's own trade/GDP fell from ~64% in 2006 to ~33% by 2016-24), **automation/robotics eroding the low-wage manufacturing ladder** (when robot cost savings offset the wage gap, production reshores to rich countries rather than offshoring to poor ones — the Rodrik/Baldwin concern), and **severe climate exposure** (**ND-GAIN ~161/182**; Lake Chad shrank from ~45,000 km² in 1960 to <3,000 km² by 2007; Sahel desertification affecting ~30m people across 15 northern states).

**Analogues at the equivalent stage.** **Korea and China caught up DURING hyper-globalization** — the world trade/GDP ratio surged from **~37% (1986) to ~61% (2008)**, and **China's 2001 WTO accession** opened vast export markets and offshoring flows *exactly as it industrialized*. Open markets and a rising tide of trade were a **tailwind**.

**Why it tilts adverse.** Tailwind then, **headwind now**: the export-manufacturing route that lifted the tigers is being **automated and reshored away before Nigeria can board it**, and climate stress hits Nigerian agriculture and the North directly, feeding factor 1 (security). **Partial offsets** keep the weight at 2 not 3: digital-services export, fintech / technological leapfrogging, and large "japa" diaspora remittances give Nigeria *some* new on-ramps that the tigers lacked — but none of these is a **mass-employment** ladder capable of absorbing a 240m-person, youthful, under-schooled labour force. They help the top of the pyramid, not the base.

## Scorecard summary

| # | Factor | Tilt | Weight | One-line reason |
|---|--------|------|:---:|---|
| 1 | Security / conflict | **adverse** | 3 | Analogues grew *after* order was restored; Nigeria's violence runs *through* its takeoff window |
| 2 | Institutions / governance | **adverse** | 3 | Corruption everywhere, but Korea's was performance-disciplined atop a capable bureaucracy; Nigeria's is extractive atop a weak one |
| 3 | Resource dependence | **adverse** | 3 | Tigers were resource-poor (forced to export manufactures); Nigeria's oil is a rentier trap (Dutch disease) |
| 4 | Demographics | **adverse now / latent-favourable** | 2 | Slow fertility fall squanders the dividend; but the youth bulge + huge home market is the one factor that could flip |
| 5 | Human capital | **adverse** | 3 | Korea/China took off literate and schooled; Nigeria has ~18m out-of-school and 74% below basic skills |
| 6 | Premature deindustrialization | **adverse** | 3 | Manufacturing ~8-9% of GDP; the escalator (Rodrik) peaks early and low — catch-up's main engine is missing |
| 7 | Global context | **adverse** | 2 | Tigers rode hyper-globalization; Nigeria faces deglobalization + automation + climate, with only partial digital offsets |

**Net conditioning read.** Weighted, the picture is **heavily adverse (six of seven, five at weight 3)**. Three of the strongest tilts — **institutions, resource dependence, and premature deindustrialization** — are also **mutually reinforcing** (oil rents weaken institutions and crowd out manufacturing), so they compound rather than average out. The **only** genuinely favourable elements are *latent, not realised*: a large young labour force and a ~240m-person domestic market (factor 4), plus digital/fintech leapfrogging and remittances (partial offset in factor 7).

**So: Korea (escape) or stall?** On these initial conditions the analogy sits **much closer to a STALL / slow-grind than to a Korea-style escape.** Korea escaped because it entered its growth window **secure, literate, resource-poor-and-therefore-export-driven, with a capable state, a fast fertility transition, and a globalizing tailwind** — Nigeria today is the near-inverse on the first five and faces a headwind on the sixth. The development-age analogy should therefore be used **only conditionally**: "Nigeria = Korea/US at the same income" holds **arithmetically** but is **structurally unearned** unless several conditions flip together — security restored, non-oil tax-and-institution build-out, a fertility acceleration, a human-capital catch-up, and a viable jobs engine (industrial or digital-services at scale). Absent those, the base case is convergence that is **slow, shallow, and vulnerable to relapse** — consistent with the middle-income-trap literature and with the macro deleveraging read in `ANALOGUE_CALIBRATION.md` ("Egypt's destination on a Turkey-length timetable"). The conditioning layer's job is precisely to stop the model from pricing a Korea outcome off a Korea-shaped income chart.
