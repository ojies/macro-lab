# Regulatory & Risk Memo: Stablecoin/Crypto + AI Financial Services for Nigeria's Informal Economy

**Prepared for:** Founder / Executive Team
**Date:** 29 June 2026
**Classification:** Confidential — Strategy & Compliance
**Subject:** Current Nigerian regulatory state, licensing pathway, FX dynamics, tax exposure, and a prioritised risk register for a crypto/stablecoin + AI venture serving the informal economy.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Nigeria has travelled, in roughly five years, from an outright banking ban on crypto (February 2021) to formal statutory recognition of digital assets as securities (Investment and Securities Act 2025) and an active — if congested — licensing regime run by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Crypto is **legal and regulated**, not prohibited. However, the operating environment is defined by a dangerous gap: the government is **taxing and supervising crypto before it has licensed the operators**. Only two exchanges (Quidax and Busha) hold provisional licences more than 18 months after the Accelerated Regulatory Incubation Programme (ARIP) opened, while a full crypto tax regime went live on 1 January 2026.

For a founder targeting the informal economy with stablecoins and AI, the single largest structural reality is **digital dollarization**: USDT alone represents roughly 88.5% of Nigerian stablecoin activity, and survey data shows the overwhelming majority of users prefer dollar-pegged value over the naira. This demand is real and durable — but it sits in direct tension with the Central Bank of Nigeria's (CBN) mandate to defend the naira and monetary sovereignty, a tension the IMF publicly amplified in June 2026. **The compliance posture must therefore be defensively over-built and the dollar-stablecoin narrative must never be framed as undermining the naira.**

## 2. Regulatory Timeline and Current State (2026)

| Date | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **5 Feb 2021** | CBN circular bars banks/OFIs from servicing crypto accounts | De-banking of the entire sector; pushed activity to P2P |
| **Oct 2021** | eNaira CBDC launched (world's 2nd after the Bahamas) | State digital-currency strategy that failed (see §2.2) |
| **22 Dec 2023** | CBN reverses ban — "Guidelines on Operations of Bank Accounts for VASPs" | Banks may service SEC-licensed VASPs; banks still barred from trading on own account |
| **Feb 2024** | Binance executives detained; EFCC charges (money laundering, tax evasion, naira "FX manipulation") | Sector-defining enforcement shock (see §2.3) |
| **Jun 2024** | SEC launches ARIP (one-year incubation sandbox) | Interim on-ramp to licensure |
| **Aug 2024** | Quidax & Busha receive provisional VASP licences | Still the only two licensed operators in 2026 |
| **Mar 2025** | President Tinubu signs **Investment and Securities Act (ISA) 2025** | Digital assets formally recognised as securities; SEC's jurisdiction codified |
| **30 Jun 2025** | SEC Digital Assets Rules / VASP Licensing Framework in force | All platforms serving Nigerians — local or foreign — must hold a VASP licence |
| **Oct 2025** | Nigeria removed from **FATF grey list**; CBN-SEC joint working group on stablecoins announced | AML credibility restored; stablecoin framework signalled |
| **1 Jan 2026** | Nigeria Tax Administration Act (NTAA) 2025 crypto provisions take effect | Full digital-asset tax regime live |
| **Jun 2026** | IMF urges Nigeria to tighten stablecoin rules over sovereignty risk | External pressure toward FSB-aligned stablecoin controls |

### 2.1 Current posture (mid-2026)
Crypto is legal, statutorily recognised, and supervised jointly by the **SEC** (market/securities conduct) and **CBN** (banking, FX, payments). But licensing is effectively **frozen**: the SEC cites "due diligence challenges," understaffing of its digital-asset desk, and a multi-agency bottleneck including security screening by the Office of the National Security Adviser (ONSA). Industry estimates 30–40+ operators active, only two licensed — a contrast with South Africa, which approved roughly 60 VASPs after launching its framework. The operative complaint from the industry is *"licence us before you tax us."*

### 2.2 eNaira: why the CBDC underperformed
The eNaira is technically live but widely regarded as a failure. IMF research found **98.5% of issued wallets had never been used**; as of March 2024 only ~₦12.53bn (~US$7.8m) was in circulation, around 0.37% of currency in circulation. Causes: distrust of CBN motives (surveillance fears), a poor early product/UX, no compelling incentive over existing wallets, and intense competition from private fintechs (OPay, PalmPay, Paga). **Lesson for the venture:** Nigerians will adopt private digital money that solves a real problem (value preservation, cheap transfers) and reject state digital money perceived as control. The cNGN regulated naira stablecoin (launched 2025) is repeating the eNaira's low-adoption pattern because users want dollar exposure, not naira exposure.

### 2.3 The Binance shock and its long tail
In February 2024 two Binance executives — Tigran Gambaryan and Nadeem Anjarwalla — were detained after a meeting with compliance officials and accused of **naira FX manipulation, money laundering, and tax evasion**. Anjarwalla escaped custody and fled; Gambaryan was held ~8 months and released in October 2024 only after money-laundering charges were dropped on medical grounds. The civil/tax dimension escalated: in February 2025 the FIRS sued Binance for roughly **US$79.5bn in claimed economic losses plus ~US$2bn in back taxes** (up from an initial US$10bn framing), asserting a "significant economic presence." A jurisdiction challenge (Binance is Cayman-domiciled) stalled proceedings, and by early 2026 the parties were reportedly negotiating a settlement. **The episode established that Nigeria will treat a large offshore crypto operator as a scapegoat for naira depreciation — a precedent every founder must internalise.**

### 2.4 Stablecoin-specific posture
There is not yet a standalone stablecoin statute. The CBN-SEC joint working group (Oct 2025) is drafting a framework; the IMF (Jun 2026) pushed for alignment with Financial Stability Board standards — issuer licensing, consumer protection, and **reporting thresholds for naira-to-digital-asset conversions**. Expect dollar-stablecoin on/off-ramps to be the first target of any new rule. The regulated naira stablecoin **cNGN** exists but is liquidity-starved.

## 3. What It Actually Takes to Operate Legally

Nigeria has no single "crypto licence." The correct stack depends on the product. A stablecoin + AI venture serving the informal economy will likely touch **three or four** of the categories below.

| Licence / Category | Regulator | What it authorises | Indicative capital / fees | Relevance to this venture |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **VASP / DAX / DAOP / DAC** | SEC | Crypto exchange, custody, offering, digital-asset services | Min. paid-up capital ~₦500m → ₦1bn (DAX/DAOP ₦1bn); registration fee historically ₦30m (reportedly rising) | **Core licence** — required for any on/off-ramp or stablecoin trading serving Nigerians |
| **ARIP (provisional)** | SEC | Time-boxed interim approval pending full VASP | Same filing/assessment as VASP + white paper | Realistic near-term on-ramp given the full-licence freeze |
| **IMTO** | CBN | Inbound international money transfers (remittances) | US$1m min. share capital (foreign) / naira equiv. (local); ₦10m application + ₦10m annual renewal | Needed if offering cross-border remittance corridors; **note: inbound only, and banks/fintechs are barred from holding the IMTO licence directly** |
| **PSP (PSSP/PSP/Switching)** | CBN | Payment processing, switching, agency banking, super-agent | Tiered (₦100m–₦2bn+ depending on category) | Needed for naira payment rails, agent networks, merchant collections |
| **MFB (Microfinance Bank)** | CBN | Deposit-taking, micro-lending to the informal sector | Tiered by tier/state/national (₦200m up to ₦5bn for national) | Relevant if offering savings/credit to informal users; deposit insurance and prudential oversight |

### KYC / AML / CTF obligations (apply across all of the above)
Following Nigeria's October 2025 FATF grey-list exit, supervisors are focused on **effectiveness**, not just paper compliance. A VASP must:
- Implement **Customer Due Diligence (CDD)**, record-keeping, and **Suspicious Transaction Reporting (STR)** to the Nigerian Financial Intelligence Unit (NFIU).
- Comply with the **FATF Travel Rule** — transmit originator/beneficiary data on transfers above the ~US$1,000 threshold.
- Under NTAA 2025, link every user account to **NIN + TIN**, file **monthly transaction reports to the FIRS**, and flag suspicious/large transactions.
- Maintain a physical office in Nigeria, a resident compliance officer, cybersecurity and data-protection controls (per ISA 2025 and the Nigeria Data Protection Act).

**Practical note:** the NIN/TIN-linking and monthly-reporting mandate is the single biggest *product-design* constraint for an informal-economy play, because much of the target market is undocumented. AI-driven identity/KYC tooling (document + liveness + alternative-data scoring) is both a compliance necessity and a genuine product wedge.

## 4. FX Controls, the Naira, and Why Stablecoins Win

The naira is the demand engine and the political fault line simultaneously.

- **The reform:** Since the June 2023 move to a "willing-buyer, willing-seller" model and the float, the naira fell from ~₦460/$ toward ₦760, breached ₦1,000 in December 2023, and lost ~37.6% in January 2024 alone (closing ~₦1,455/$). Reserves nonetheless rose from ~US$35bn (2021) to >US$43bn (2025).
- **Convergence:** The float largely closed the official-vs-parallel gap. Tellingly, the **crypto premium over the official rate collapsed from ~65% (pre-float) to ~6% (post-float)** — direct evidence that crypto/stablecoins had been pricing the *real* (parallel) exchange rate all along.
- **Why this drives stablecoin demand:** With the naira having lost ~70%+ of its value over a decade, USDT/USDC are the informal economy's de-facto dollar savings account and cross-border settlement layer. This is structural, not speculative.
- **The tension:** The CBN views dollar-stablecoins as a vector for **"digital dollarization"** that erodes monetary-policy transmission and pressures the naira — exactly the framing used against Binance. A future FX-policy reversal (return to multiple windows, capital controls, or restrictions on naira→stablecoin conversion) is a live risk (see §6).

## 5. Tax Exposure

The **Nigeria Tax Administration Act (NTAA) 2025**, effective **1 January 2026**, brings digital assets fully into the net (Nigeria processed ~US$92bn in crypto volume, Jul 2024–Jun 2025).

- **Gains on disposal of digital assets** are treated as chargeable gains under the income-tax framework — reporting variously cites effective rates up to **25–30%**, replacing the old flat 10% capital-gains treatment.
- **Taxable events** include trading, transfers, mining, staking, airdrops, and crypto-denominated compensation.
- **Intermediary-led enforcement:** rather than chasing the blockchain, the law deputises exchanges/VASPs as collection and reporting agents — monthly FIRS filings, NIN/TIN linkage, NFIU flagging.
- **Penalties** are severe: up to **200%** of amounts involved (plus possible imprisonment) for tax offences, and **₦10m for the first month of non-compliance, then ₦1m/month** for VASP reporting failures.
- **Informal-sector reality:** the policy goal is *formalisation* — pulling previously untracked activity into the tax net via the platforms. For a venture serving informal users, this creates friction (users resist documentation/withholding) but also a moat (compliant rails become the only legal way to transact at scale).

## 6. Risk Register

Likelihood (L) and Impact (I) scored 1–5 (5 = highest). Priority = L × I.

| # | Risk | L | I | Priority | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | **Regulatory whipsaw** — licence freeze, sudden rule changes, ban-then-reverse pattern repeats | 5 | 5 | **25** | Pursue ARIP/VASP filing now to be "in the queue"; design product to degrade gracefully (non-custodial fallbacks); maintain a regulatory-affairs lead; keep a second-jurisdiction entity (e.g. Rwanda, SA, Mauritius) so the company survives a Nigeria pause |
| R2 | **Reputational / "Binance-scapegoat" risk** — being blamed for naira weakness; founder detention precedent | 4 | 5 | **20** | Never market dollar-stablecoins as anti-naira; foreground remittances, financial inclusion, tax-compliance, and naira-rail products; build CBN/SEC relationships *before* scale; keep executives' travel/exposure managed; engage local counsel and a government-affairs advisor |
| R3 | **De-banking** — banks fine/close crypto-linked accounts despite legality | 4 | 4 | **16** | Bank only through partners comfortable with SEC-licensed VASP accounts (per Dec 2023 guidelines); diversify across 2–3 banking partners; obtain licence to unlock compliant banking; consider MFB/PSP rails to internalise settlement |
| R4 | **FX-policy reversal / capital controls** — restrictions on naira→stablecoin conversion | 3 | 5 | **15** | Stress-test the model against on/off-ramp restrictions; diversify into remittance (inbound, IMTO-aligned) and naira-native payment revenue; avoid dependence on a single conversion corridor |
| R5 | **Stablecoin de-peg / issuer risk** — USDT/USDC/cNGN failure or freeze | 3 | 5 | **15** | Diversify across reputable issuers (USDC's attestations, regulated cNGN for naira leg); avoid algorithmic stablecoins; hold minimal float; clear user disclosures; real-time peg monitoring with auto-halt |
| R6 | **AML/fraud exposure** — informal users, weak KYC, money-laundering liability | 4 | 4 | **16** | Over-invest in CDD, Travel Rule, NFIU/FIRS reporting; deploy AI transaction-monitoring and identity verification; appoint a resident compliance officer; tiered limits for low-KYC users; independent AML audit |
| R7 | **Tax non-compliance** — NTAA monthly reporting / NIN-TIN failures | 3 | 4 | **12** | Build automated FIRS reporting and NIN/TIN capture into onboarding from day one; treat tax-reporting tooling as core infrastructure, not an afterthought |
| R8 | **Capital-requirement escalation** — ₦1bn+ paid-up capital prices out entry | 3 | 3 | **9** | Budget for the ₦1bn capital line; lobby (via industry body) for tiered/start-up licences; consider ARIP route and partnerships with an already-licensed operator |
| R9 | **AI/model & data-protection risk** — NDPA breaches, biased credit scoring, hallucinated financial advice | 3 | 3 | **9** | NDPA-compliant data handling; human-in-the-loop for credit/financial decisions; model governance, audit logs, and clear disclaimers; avoid unlicensed "investment advice" framing |

**Top three by priority: R1 (regulatory whipsaw), R2 (scapegoat/reputational), R3/R6 (de-banking & AML).**

## 7. Recommended Compliance Posture

1. **Get into the regulatory queue immediately.** File the SEC initial assessment and pursue **ARIP/provisional VASP** status now. Even with the freeze, being a known, cooperating applicant is materially safer than operating unlicensed — the tax regime is already enforcing against unlicensed operators.
2. **Build compliance as product, not overhead.** NIN/TIN capture, FIRS monthly reporting, Travel Rule data, and NFIU STR pipelines should be native infrastructure from launch. Use AI for KYC/identity and transaction monitoring — this doubles as the informal-economy onboarding wedge.
3. **Run a defensive narrative.** Position as **financial inclusion, remittances, and tax-compliant rails** — never as a dollar-store that weakens the naira. Pre-empt the Binance precedent through proactive CBN/SEC engagement and a local government-affairs presence.
4. **Engineer jurisdictional and banking resilience.** Maintain a second-country entity and 2–3 banking relationships; design non-custodial fallbacks so a sudden policy pause does not freeze user funds.
5. **Manage stablecoin and peg risk conservatively.** Diversify issuers (USDC + regulated cNGN for the naira leg), avoid algorithmic stablecoins, minimise float, and instrument real-time peg/auto-halt controls.
6. **Capitalise realistically.** Plan for the ₦1bn paid-up capital threshold and ₦30m+ fees; if undercapitalised, partner with a licensed VASP rather than operate in the grey.
7. **Protect the people.** Given the detention precedent, manage executive exposure, retain top-tier Nigerian counsel, and keep board-level oversight of regulatory and AML risk.

**Bottom line:** The opportunity is real and the legal door is open, but the regime is mid-construction and politically charged. Win by being the *most compliant, most cooperative, most naira-friendly* operator in the room — not the fastest or the cheapest.
